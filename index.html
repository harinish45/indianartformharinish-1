KOLAM & RANGOLI

Where the floor becomes a canvas and every morning a prayer — two sister traditions of Indian floor art, drawn in rice flour and coloured powder to welcome the divine home.

Tamil · Kerala · Karnataka

Kolam (கோலம்)

Drawn daily at dawn on the red-earth threshold in rice flour. Never finished — erased by feet and wind, redrawn each morning: an art of impermanence and devotion.

  • Grid of pulled dots (pulli) joined by one unbroken line
  • White on red; coloured powders for festivals
  • Freehand, no sketching — memory in the wrist
  • Welcome, prosperity, feminine skill
Pan-India

Rangoli (रंगोली)

From ranga (colour) + avali (row): colourful floor art in powders, petals, rice or sand — alpana in Bengal, mandana in Rajasthan, muggu in Andhra, pookalam in Kerala.

  • Floral, geometric or figurative motifs
  • Turmeric, kumkum, indigo, neem, ochre
  • Diwali, Onam, Pongal, weddings
  • Invites Lakshmi, wards off the inauspicious
Cultural meaning

Threshold, Community, Ecology

The vaasal is sacred boundary; the kolam consecrates it. Patterns pass mother to daughter as an oral-visual library. The rice flour feeds ants and birds — beauty that gives back before it is erased.

  • Daily sadhana: the day begun with intention
  • Festival mornings turn streets into galleries
  • Continuous loops = Eulerian mathematics, intuited
Religious meaning

Lakshmi In, Alakshmi Out

A fresh kolam tells Lakshmi the home is ready; lamps set in Diwali rangoli guide her in. The unbroken line wards off Alakshmi. The radiating chakka-pulli is a floor-bound yantra: bindu = source, petals = creation, erasure = return.

  • Geometry as prayer, symmetry as order
  • Every dot enclosed = no blessing left out
